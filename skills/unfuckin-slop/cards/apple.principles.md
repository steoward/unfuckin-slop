# Eight principles

Use these as the names you reason with. They are the "why" behind every number in the other cards.

1. **Purpose.** Make with intention; decide what *not* to build. Every feature spends the user's time, attention, and trust.
2. **Agency.** Keep people in control. Offer choices, don't force one path. Back it with forgiveness: easy undo, confirmations only for genuinely destructive and irreversible actions.
3. **Responsibility.** Act in the user's interest. Ask for privacy at the moment it is needed, only for what is needed. Anticipate misuse, especially with AI output.
4. **Familiarity.** Build on what people already know. Metaphors neither too literal nor too abstract, with honest physics. Things that look the same behave the same, in the same place.
5. **Flexibility.** Design for contexts, devices, and the full range of abilities. Adapt to the platform (thumb and touch vs. pointer and deep workflows). Let people personalise when one layout cannot fit everyone.
6. **Simplicity, not minimalism.** Strip the unnecessary so the core purpose shows. Concise (plain language, fewer steps) and clear (hierarchy so the most important thing is most obvious). Sometimes *adding* context simplifies.
7. **Craft.** Nothing random: every spacing, timing, and alignment value is defensible. Jittery scroll, misaligned icons, and broken rotation read as carelessness.
8. **Delight.** The result of getting the other seven right, not confetti on top. Name the emotion you want (calm, confident, excited) and reinforce it everywhere.

# Tactical rules

- **Feedback has four kinds:** status, completion, warning, error. Confirm meaningful actions, expose ongoing status, warn before problems, validate inline rather than on submit.
- **Wayfinding.** Every screen answers: Where am I? Where can I go? What is there? How do I get out? Never trap the user.
- **Grouping and mapping.** Proximity implies relationship. Put a control near what it affects, and arrange controls to mirror what they change. If a label is needed to explain a control, the mapping is weak.
- **Specific labels beat safe ones.** Name navigation for its contents ("Progress", "Library"), not vague umbrellas ("Home"). Specificity creates predictability.

# Smell test

If a screen cannot state its purpose in one sentence, it is two screens or none. If you cannot name which of the eight principles the next change serves, you are decorating.
